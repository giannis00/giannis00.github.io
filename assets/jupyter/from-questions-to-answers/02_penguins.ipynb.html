The Palmer Penguins dataset is the modern replacement for the old Iris flowers: small, clean enough to start with, messy enough to be honest, and full of patterns that are easy to see once you know where to look.

0. The question¶

Between 2007 and 2009, Dr Kristen Gorman and the Palmer Station Long Term Ecological Research programme in Antarctica measured penguins of three species on three islands. For each bird we know its species, island, sex, body mass and three measurements of its bill and flipper.

Our question: Can we tell which species a penguin is from its measurements? More precisely:

  1. Which measurements differ between species, and which are almost the same?
  2. Would the species be visible in the measurements even if nobody had labelled them?
  3. What is the smallest set of measurements a field researcher would need?

What we see. Each measurement separates the species in a different way:

  • Flipper length and body mass separate Gentoo from the other two: Gentoo are much bigger (mean flipper 217 mm and mass 5,092 g, against about 190 mm and 3,700 g), but Adelie and Chinstrap overlap almost completely.

No single measurement separates all three species. But two measurements that fail in different places might succeed together. That is the idea behind looking at pairs.

What we see. A pair plot draws every pair of measurements as a scatter plot, with the distribution of each measurement on the diagonal. Look for panels where the three colours form three separate clouds:

  • Bill length against flipper length (and against bill depth) shows three clear groups.
  • Flipper length against body mass shows only two: Gentoo, and everyone else mixed together.

So the answer to "which pair of measurements identifies the species?" is already visible: bill length, combined with either flipper length or bill depth.

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 4))
sns.regplot(data=df, x="bill_length_mm", y="bill_depth_mm", ax=axes[0], scatter_kws={"s": 12}, line_kws={"color": "black"})
axes[0].set_title("All penguins together: a negative trend")
sns.scatterplot(data=df, x="bill_length_mm", y="bill_depth_mm", hue="species", s=15, ax=axes[1])
for species, group in df.groupby("species"):
    sns.regplot(data=group, x="bill_length_mm", y="bill_depth_mm", scatter=False, ax=axes[1])
axes[1].set_title("Within each species: a positive trend")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

What we see. Over all penguins, the correlation between bill length and bill depth is -0.23: longer bills seem to be shallower. But within every single species the correlation is positive: +0.39 for Adelie, +0.65 for Chinstrap and Gentoo. Within a species, a bigger bird simply has a bigger bill in every direction, as you would expect.

The overall trend is negative only because the species sit in different corners: Gentoo have long and shallow bills, Adelie short and deep ones. Mixing groups reversed the true relationship. This is Simpson's paradox, and it is the reason to always ask whether your data contains groups before trusting a correlation. In the Titanic notebook, class hid behind the port; here, species hides behind the bill.

5. Would the species appear without labels?¶

Intuition first¶

Suppose nobody had told us the species. Could an algorithm discover three groups on its own? k-means tries to split the points into k groups so that each point is close to the centre of its group. It knows nothing about species: we only compare its groups with the true labels afterwards.

But "close" is measured in the original units, and here body mass is in grams (values around 4,000) while bills are in millimetres (values around 15 to 50). A difference of 500 g would count far more than a difference of 10 mm, although 10 mm in a bill is huge. Scaling each measurement to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 gives every measurement an equal voice.

In [13]:
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.metrics import adjusted_rand_score

X = df[measures]
raw_groups = KMeans(n_clusters=3, n_init=10, random_state=42).fit_predict(X)
scaled_groups = KMeans(n_clusters=3, n_init=10, random_state=42).fit_predict(StandardScaler().fit_transform(X))

print("Agreement with the true species (adjusted Rand index, 1 = perfect, 0 = chance)")
print("  without scaling:", round(adjusted_rand_score(df["species"], raw_groups), 2))
print("  with scaling:   ", round(adjusted_rand_score(df["species"], scaled_groups), 2))
pd.crosstab(df["species"], scaled_groups, colnames=["cluster (scaled)"])

Agreement with the true species (adjusted Rand index, 1 = perfect, 0 = chance)
  without scaling: 0.32
  with scaling:    0.8

What we see.

  • Without scaling, the agreement with the true species is only 0.32: k-means split the birds mostly by body mass, the measurement with the biggest numbers.
  • With scaling, the agreement jumps to 0.80. The table shows that one cluster is exactly the 119 Gentoo, a second holds 124 of the 146 Adelie, and a third holds 63 of the 68 Chinstrap together with 22 Adelie.

So yes: the species are visible in the measurements without any labels, as long as the measurements are put on the same scale. The remaining confusion is between Adelie and Chinstrap, the pair that the box plots already showed to be similar in size.

In [14]:
inertia = [KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=10, random_state=42).fit(StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)).inertia_ for k in range(1, 8)]
plt.figure(figsize=(6, 3.5))
plt.plot(range(1, 8), inertia, marker="o")
plt.xlabel("number of clusters k")
plt.ylabel("within-cluster spread")
plt.title("The elbow method")
plt.show()

What we see. If we did not know there were three species, how would we choose k? The elbow method plots how tightly packed the clusters are against k. The spread falls steeply up to about 3 and more slowly after: the "elbow" suggests 2 or 3 groups. The elbow is a hint, not a proof; here it agrees reasonably with biology, but on many datasets it is not clear at all.

6. The smallest set of measurements¶

Now we use the labels, and ask how much each measurement is worth. We train a logistic regression on different sets of measurements and score it with 5-fold cross-validation.

In [15]:
from sklearn.model_selection import StratifiedKFold, cross_val_score
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

y = df["species"]
cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=42)
model = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000))

candidates = {
    "flipper length only": ["flipper_length_mm"],
    "bill length only": ["bill_length_mm"],
    "bill length + flipper length": ["bill_length_mm", "flipper_length_mm"],
    "bill length + bill depth": ["bill_length_mm", "bill_depth_mm"],
    "all four measurements": measures,
}
pd.Series({name: cross_val_score(model, df[cols], y, cv=cv).mean() for name, cols in candidates.items()}, name="accuracy").to_frame()
Out[15]:
accuracy
flipper length only 0.799
bill length only 0.748
bill length + flipper length 0.955
bill length + bill depth 0.958
all four measurements 0.988

What we see.

  • One measurement is not enough: flipper length gives 80% (it finds Gentoo but mixes the other two), bill length 75%.
  • Two well-chosen measurements give about 95%: bill length with flipper length, or with bill depth.
  • All four give 99%.

All far above the 43.8% baseline. For a field researcher, two measurements with a ruler already identify the species almost every time, which is exactly what the pair plot suggested.

from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier, plot_tree

tree = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=2, random_state=42).fit(df[["bill_length_mm", "flipper_length_mm"]], y)
print("Cross-validated accuracy of a two-question tree:",
      round(cross_val_score(DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=2, random_state=42), df[["bill_length_mm", "flipper_length_mm"]], y, cv=cv).mean(), 3))
plt.figure(figsize=(10, 4.5))
plot_tree(tree, feature_names=["bill_length_mm", "flipper_length_mm"], class_names=tree.classes_, filled=True, impurity=False)
plt.show()

Cross-validated accuracy of a two-question tree: 0.934

What we see. A tree that asks only two questions reaches 93%. Its rules can be written on a card: if the flipper is longer than about 206 mm, it is a Gentoo (unless the bill is very short); otherwise, a bill longer than about 43 mm means Chinstrap, and a shorter one means Adelie. A slightly less accurate model that a person can carry in their head is often more useful than a perfect one they cannot.

7. Answer to the question¶

Can we tell which species a penguin is from its measurements?

  1. Yes, easily: four measurements identify the species 99% of the time, and two well-chosen ones about 95%.
  2. Each measurement separates different species: size (flipper, mass) picks out Gentoo, bill length picks out Adelie, so the best pairs combine bill length with a size or shape measurement.
  3. The species are visible without labels: k-means rediscovers them with an agreement of 0.80, but only after scaling; without scaling, the grams drown out the millimetres.
  4. Beware mixed groups: bill length and depth look negatively related over all penguins and are positively related within every species (Simpson's paradox), and sex differences in mass are larger than some species differences.
  5. Island is not a measurement: it predicts species almost perfectly, but only because of where each species lives.

🚀 Going further (for experienced students)¶

A. How sure can we be about the number of clusters?¶

The elbow is a visual hint. The silhouette score measures, for every point, how much closer it is to its own cluster than to the nearest other one: near 1 means well separated, near 0 means on a border.

In [17]:
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score

scaled = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)
silhouette = {k: silhouette_score(scaled, KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=10, random_state=42).fit_predict(scaled)) for k in range(2, 7)}
pd.Series(silhouette, name="silhouette").round(3).to_frame().T
Out[17]:
2 3 4 5 6
silhouette 0.531 0.446 0.398 0.374 0.364

What we see. The silhouette is highest for k = 2 (0.53), then 0.45 for k = 3. On numbers alone, the data says two groups (Gentoo and the rest) more clearly than three, because Adelie and Chinstrap overlap in size. Biology says three. A cluster count chosen by a score describes the geometry of the data, not necessarily the categories that matter.

B. Drawing the decision boundaries¶

A model with two inputs can be drawn: colour every point of the plane by the species the model would predict there.

In [18]:
two = ["bill_length_mm", "bill_depth_mm"]
clf = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000)).fit(df[two], y)
xx, yy = np.meshgrid(np.linspace(df[two[0]].min() - 2, df[two[0]].max() + 2, 300),
                     np.linspace(df[two[1]].min() - 1, df[two[1]].max() + 1, 300))
grid = pd.DataFrame({two[0]: xx.ravel(), two[1]: yy.ravel()})
zone = pd.Series(clf.predict(grid)).map({s: i for i, s in enumerate(clf.classes_)}).values.reshape(xx.shape)
plt.figure(figsize=(7, 5))
plt.contourf(xx, yy, zone, alpha=0.2, levels=[-0.5, 0.5, 1.5, 2.5], colors=["tab:blue", "tab:orange", "tab:green"])
sns.scatterplot(data=df, x=two[0], y=two[1], hue="species", hue_order=list(clf.classes_),
                palette=["tab:blue", "tab:orange", "tab:green"], s=15)
plt.title("Logistic regression regions on two bill measurements")
plt.show()

What we see. With only bill length and bill depth, three straight-edged regions separate the species well: Adelie top left (short, deep bills), Chinstrap top right (long, deep), Gentoo at the bottom (long, shallow). The few points in the wrong colour region are the birds the model gets wrong, mostly near the Adelie and Chinstrap border. Logistic regression can only draw straight borders; a tree would draw rectangles, and k nearest neighbours irregular shapes.

⚠️ Common pitfalls¶

  • Trusting a pooled correlation when the data contains groups. The bill correlation changed sign once species was taken into account.
  • Using distances on unscaled data. k-means, k-nearest neighbours and any method that measures distance will be dominated by the variable with the largest numbers.
  • Letting a "shortcut" variable in. island predicts species, but for the wrong reason; in a new place, it would fail completely.
  • Reading the elbow as a rule. It suggests a number of clusters; it does not prove one.
  • Ignoring class balance. With 44% Adelie, an accuracy of 50% would be barely better than always guessing Adelie.

🔑 Key takeaways¶

  • Measurements are explored with box plots per group, then pair plots, looking for panels where groups form separate clouds.
  • Different measurements separate different groups; combinations succeed where single variables fail.
  • Simpson's paradox: a trend in the whole dataset can reverse inside every group. Always ask what groups your data contains.
  • Scale your features before any method based on distance.
  • Clustering can rediscover real groups without labels, and comparing clusters with known labels tells you how real they are.
  • Keep out variables that predict the answer for the wrong reason, such as where the data was collected.
